Subject: Two missing variables in long‑range demand‑forecast uncertainty: booked‑capacity growth and climate change

To NESO's Future Energy Scenarios team (box.fes@neso.energy),

I've recently submitted an independent, evidence‑based response to Ofgem's Curate consultation on data‑centre connections and queue management, in a personal capacity rather than on behalf of any developer, DNO, vendor, or other stakeholder. Two findings sit squarely within your remit rather than Ofgem's — one about what the network data actually shows, one about how you frame uncertainty itself — so I'm writing directly rather than leaving them in a consultation return.

The first concerns the demand side of your own growth range directly. We analysed UK Power Networks' own open data on data‑centre grid connections — 96 sites, 5.4 million half-hourly readings — and found a large, persistent gap between contracted capacity and what's actually drawn: for every 100MW booked, only around 20MW is drawn on average — a per-site figure, not a portfolio-level coincidence effect. Even at each site's own busiest half-hour in three years, the median site still drew only 41% of what it had booked. The gap is widest at the largest, Extra-High Voltage sites. If demand growth is modelled from the connections queue or from booked capacity, that gap is worth a second look — the queue's growth may not translate into grid demand at anything like a 1:1 ratio. That growth rate is itself hard to verify: one DNO confirmed an apparent ~40% six-month jump in their own published pipeline figure was genuine growth, not a methodology artefact — but also that they hold no historical snapshots to check trends like this themselves. It took an outside before/after comparison to catch it. I'd welcome clarity on this specifically: does FES apply any utilisation or diversity discount when translating connections-queue growth into demand growth, or is booked capacity treated as a reasonable proxy for what's eventually drawn?

The second concerns how you frame uncertainty itself. Your published long‑range data‑centre demand range, the 30–71 TWh range by 2050, appears to attribute that uncertainty entirely to market growth: how many data centres are built in the UK versus other competitive locations. I haven't been able to find climate‑change‑driven heatwave frequency, cooling‑mode switching, or water‑stress‑driven planning outcomes named anywhere in that list of uncertainty drivers.

That gap matters because the variable you're not modelling isn't controlled by operators. It sits with local planning authorities, who decide whether a data centre cools with water or with air. That choice already changes electrical demand, especially in high ambient temperatures. Even "closed-loop" systems, now common for AI compute, don't remove the effect: they run dry for most of the year, then switch on evaporative assist once temperature rises, or simply because water is the cheaper or more convenient option regardless of the weather — either way, trading water for a lower electricity draw, since water absorbs heat more efficiently than air. Climate change will increase the frequency and severity of exactly those periods over the horizon your scenarios cover. If a rising share of the pipeline sits on one side or the other of this water-electricity trade-off, and heatwave frequency rises over the same window, that is a structural pressure on demand that your current methodology does not appear to carry an explicit variable for — separate from, and additional to, the market-growth uncertainty you already model.

I don't have access to your internal FES methodology, so I can't tell whether either factor has been considered and deliberately excluded, or simply hasn't yet been surfaced given how recent these dynamics are. I would genuinely welcome clarity on which is the case.

Ofgem's Climate Resilience Report (ARP4, April 2025) notes that "clarity is required on overall resilience across key infrastructure and how regulators work together" (§5.5). The cooling/climate gap above looks like a concrete instance of that coordination gap, and one that sits with NESO rather than Ofgem.

I'd be happy to share the fuller evidence base — including the underlying utilisation analysis, DNO correspondence on pipeline growth, parliamentary written evidence from Energy UK, Global Action Plan, and Water UK on the water/cooling/planning misalignment, and the peer-reviewed research behind the free-cooling and water-energy trade-off points above — if it would be useful input to how FES treats these uncertainties going forward.

Many thanks for your time, and for the work that goes into FES.
