The data centre water debate has become a proxy war fought mostly with vibes. One side points at drought headlines and treats every new facility as an act of theft from the local reservoir. The other points at cooling-loop diagrams and declares the question solved. One side is anxious about the hosepipe; the other has a PR budget. The engineering reality sits between them and it matters precisely because the industry's version currently carries more weight. Neither side is doing the sector any favours, but the side with the PR budget is the more dangerous one right now.

## The current debate and GB drought

Roughly 71.3% of England was in drought on 10 August 2026.[^1] We've had three droughts in five years, with hosepipe bans and irrigation restrictions covering more than 27 million people this year. This is the environment new data centre capacity is being sited into, right now.

The people saying the water math doesn't add up aren't an uninformed public. It's Water UK, the trade body for England and Northern Ireland's water companies. They state plainly that the government's AI Opportunities Action Plan, AI for Science Strategy and Delivering AI Growth Zones policy documents "make not a single mention of water."[^2] The government's official forecasts, they note, "explicitly exclude" data centres.[^2] As Water UK put it: "There appears to be an assumption that the country will always have enough water for its economic needs. Nothing could be further from the truth."[^2] Thames Water has formally objected to specific data centre proposals on supply-capacity grounds. Independent analysis by Global Action Plan, submitted as written evidence to Parliament, found that 84% of proposed UK data centres are planned for water-stressed areas.[^3] The government's own commissioned report corroborates the geographic overlap, noting that the country's first AI Growth Zone (Culham, Oxfordshire) sits roughly seven miles from a reservoir planned specifically to serve an already "seriously water stressed" Thames Valley and London.[^4]

The same report also flags a more basic problem: nobody has good data. Only two in five operators actively track water metrics, and there's no mandatory location-specific reporting threshold. You cannot regulate, or even seriously debate, what you don't measure. The same measurement gap means local communities and planning authorities are being asked to assess proposals without the data to do so. This is why the debate gets reduced to vibes. The vibes are a symptom, not the cause.

## "Sealed loop, nothing evaporates" is true and incomplete

The most common rebuttal to water concern is a real engineering fact stated as settled: single-phase direct-to-chip cooling loops are sealed and charged once, so they don't evaporate. Which is right. But it's not the number that determines a site's water footprint.

Picture your car's radiator. The coolant circulating through the engine block is sealed too — you don't top it up weekly, nothing boils off inside that loop. But the engine bay still has a radiator core with a fan blowing outside air across it, because that sealed loop still has to dump its heat somewhere. Whether that costs you water depends entirely on what sits on the other side of the radiator, not on whether the loop touching the engine is sealed.

Data centre cooling works the same way. The chip-level loop being sealed only settles what happens at the chip. The facility still has to reject that heat to the outside world, and the mechanism it uses for that — not the sealed loop — is what determines the site's actual water number. A dry cooler rejects heat with fans over finned coils and uses close to zero water. An evaporative or adiabatic cooling tower rejects the same heat by evaporating water, because evaporation is a far more efficient way to dump heat, especially in hot weather — which is exactly why operators reach for it: it lowers PUE and opex.

Water Usage Effectiveness (WUE, litres of water per kWh of IT energy) varies by close to an order of magnitude depending on this one choice. An evaporatively-cooled facility typically consumes on the order of 1.8 litres of water per kWh of IT energy.[^5] By contrast, facilities that commit to dry or hybrid rejection report figures like 0.19–0.20 L/kWh.[^6] That's not a rounding error. One design decision downstream of the chip's sealed loop sets the facility's water profile.

So the "closed loop, nothing evaporates" claim is uncontroversial engineering. A more honest question though is: what does the facility-side heat rejection loop do, and under what ambient conditions does it switch modes? Plenty of "closed loop" marketing copy answers the first question and lets you assume the answer to the second.

## Training vs inference: real distinction, wrong conclusion

Inference is the faster-growing share of AI compute. Where this breaks is treating inference as a reason the water question is receding.

It isn't, for two reasons. First, "faster-growing share" doesn't mean training capacity shrinks — the same forecast has training growing at a 22% CAGR over the same period. Absolute training-driven demand is still nearly tripling. Second, and more importantly: today's highest-profile water controversies aren't a hangover from some earlier, dying category of building.

The pattern isn't unique to the UK. Google's Dalles, Oregon campus accounts for roughly 40% of that city's total water use and is currently the subject of a contested federal bill to expand the town's reservoir onto national forest land specifically to accommodate it.[^7] xAI's Colossus campus in Memphis was described by CNBC in July 2026 as "the epicentre of the data centre backlash."[^8] These are live, current, training-and-inference-mixed hyperscale sites, not artefacts of a receding building type. The share of the pie inference occupies is a real trend; it isn't evidence that the water question is being engineered away.

## It's not only water. It's "siting and design" too

Framing this as purely a water problem actually understates the case, because the full siting-and-design bundle includes at least four constraints that don't disappear just because the cooling loop is sealed.

**Grid capacity.** The UK's grid connection queue has historically ballooned past 700 GW of largely speculative demand; reforms in 2026 cut that to a firmer 238 GW pipeline through 2035 by prioritising projects with genuine readiness, and data centres now get treated as critical national infrastructure with priority access.[^9] Even so, only around 13 GW of new firm demand is expected to connect before 2030, with a further 86 GW pushed into 2030–2035 — meaning most of the demand growth people are building business cases around today is backloaded by half a decade or more, and typical connection timelines still run 5–10 years.[^9]

In London specifically, the Greater London Authority has said grid connection requests from data centres already run at roughly 10x current grid capacity in the capital.[^10] Distributed inference sites sited close to users don't escape this — if anything, they concentrate new electrical and thermal load onto local, often already-constrained urban substations rather than a purpose-built campus with its own dedicated connection.

**Urban Heat: The Sibling Problem to Water.** That word "thermal" deserves unpacking on its own, because urban siting isn't only a grid problem — it's a local heat problem too, and one that compounds with the water question rather than sitting apart from it.

UK cities already run meaningfully hotter than their surroundings before a data centre arrives: Arup's Urban Heat Snapshot study found London joint-fourth most extreme of six major cities surveyed, 4.5°C hotter than rural surroundings at night.[^14]

A dry-cooled facility adds to that baseline directly. Field measurements from Arizona State University researchers across four Phoenix-area data centres (36–169 MW) found downwind temperatures averaging 1.3–1.6°F warmer than upwind readings, with peak measurements reaching 4°F (roughly 0.7–2.2°C), detectable up to a third of a mile — roughly five city blocks — from the site perimeter, from air-cooled condenser systems discharging air 14–25°F above ambient. The waste heat from a single facility in the study was reported as comparable to that produced by around 40,000 households.[^15]

Here's the wrinkle worth sitting with: dry cooling rejects heat almost entirely as sensible heat, which simply raises the temperature of the surrounding air. Evaporative cooling rejects a meaningful share of that same heat as latent heat instead, carried away in water vapour rather than warming the local air nearly as much — the same physics behind why urban greening and evaporative surfaces are the standard tool for cooling cities down. Dry-first design, the lower-water option praised elsewhere in this piece, is also the option that puts more raw heat directly into the local air. For a distributed inference site deliberately sited close to users, that's added warming on top of a UHI baseline that's already among the most severe of any major city, in exactly the dense urban locations distributed inference is supposed to favour, and exactly the kind of local heat that pushes a site toward the adiabatic-assist threshold sooner.

**Fire-water provisioning.** This is a genuine, distinct constraint from operational cooling water. UK fire and rescue authorities operate under a statutory duty (Fire and Rescue Services Act 2004) to secure an adequate water supply for firefighting; water undertakers have obligations to provide hydrants under the Water Industry Act 1991; Building Regulations Approved Document B sets functional requirements that Building Control applies case by case; and industry practice is set out in the NFCC/Water UK National Guidance Document on the Provision of Water for Firefighting (4th edition, May 2024).[^11]

That guidance is explicit that mains water systems are not designed to meet firefighting demand on their own, and points operators toward on-site static storage tanks, dedicated fire mains or nearby watercourses as the practical solution for large sites. None of that is resolved by a sealed IT cooling loop; it's a separate, site-specific engineering and planning question that sits alongside cooling water, not underneath it.

**Data transparency.** Underlying all of the above is the measurement gap the government's own report flags: without mandatory, location-specific reporting, water resource planning (which happens through statutory Water Resources Management Plans) can't actually account for data centre demand, because nobody submitting those plans has reliable numbers to work with.

## What's genuinely working, and shouldn't get lost

None of this is an argument that the sector is behaving badly. Heat reuse is a real, deployed answer to part of this problem. Deep Green's Trafford Leisure Centre partnership in Manchester captures waste heat from a data centre serving racks up to 150kW and uses it to warm a public swimming pool, saving the centre an estimated £80,000 a year and cutting the carbon cost of heat that would otherwise be rejected to atmosphere for nothing.[^12] That's what we need more of.

Dry-cooler-first design, hybrid rejection that only switches to evaporative under genuine peak-demand stress, and non-potable or greywater sourcing for the water that is used are all real levers. Operators who lead with dry rejection numbers like 0.19–0.20 L/kWh are demonstrably not the same risk profile as the industry average.

At the same time, even dry-first designs aren't a permanent guarantee. Dry rejection holds a low WUE by moving heat into the air with fans alone; push the ambient temperature high enough and that stops being sufficient to hold capacity, which is precisely why so many "dry" installs are actually hybrid systems with adiabatic assist built in, ready to engage when the air alone can't take the heat. The exact trigger point is design-specific — it varies by vendor, redundancy margin and rated equipment class — but the direction of travel is the same everywhere: as hotter days become more frequent rather than exceptional, the number of hours a year that assist is running goes up, and water use that a site's headline WUE figure implied was solved starts coming back. A published 0.19–0.20 L/kWh figure is a true snapshot, not a permanent guarantee, unless it's paired with disclosure of how much assist-mode operation is baked into that number and how that's expected to shift as design-basis climate assumptions age.

That's not a hypothetical future test — it's already showing up in this year's weather. By 15 July 2026, the UK had recorded more days above 30°C than the whole of 1976, one of the country's most notorious hot, dry summers. Provisional Met Office data shows 2026 became the first year on record with 35°C-plus heat in May, June and July alike, including a run of eight days at or above 34°C that broke the previous record jointly held by 1976 and 2020.[^13] The underlying trend behind that single hot year is steeper still: across the last decade, days over 30°C in Greater London have been roughly four times higher than across the 1961–1990 baseline, a period in which the UK failed to reach 30°C anywhere in around one year in five.[^13] Every one of those extra hours above the threshold where dry rejection alone can't hold capacity is an hour a site either burns significantly more energy holding the line with fans — power draw scales with the cube of fan speed by the fan affinity laws (a doubling of speed means eight times the power) — or brings adiabatic assist back online and starts using water again. That's not a design-basis assumption due for revision at some point in the future. It's this summer.

## The actual conclusion

"It's a siting and design question, not a reason to be nervous about the technology" isn't wrong so much as it answers its own question incorrectly. Siting and design are where the legitimate nervousness comes from — not from silicon, not from the chip-level loop, but from where a facility is built, what it uses to reject heat when the loop it's connected to gets hot, how that answer holds up as the climate it was designed against keeps shifting, how it connects to an already-strained grid, and how it provisions water for the fire risk that comes with any dense electrical load. Those are exactly the variables that are currently under-measured, under-reported, and — per the UK's own water industry regulator-adjacent trade body — not adequately accounted for in the government's own AI growth strategy.

The site's actual water footprint — and its future footprint — depends on three things: the facility-side heat rejection architecture, the local water stress classification, and the fire-water provisioning. Not the sealed loop, not the chip-level technology — those are just the delivery truck. The engineering distinctions getting waved around in this debate are all real and worth knowing. None of them, individually or together, tells you what a given site's water footprint actually is, and none of them is a one-time fix. Dry-first design lowers the number today, but as hotter days stop being exceptional, that number is only as durable as the assist-mode assumptions behind it. Only the facility-side heat rejection architecture (and how it's expected to behave under a warmer design-basis climate, not just today's), the local water stress classification, and the site's fire-water provisioning tell you what a site's water footprint actually is and will become — and that's precisely the information the current debate is short on. The future this points to isn't water-free cooling; it's hybrid cooling, honestly disclosed.
