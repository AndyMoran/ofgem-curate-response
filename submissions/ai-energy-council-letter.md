Subject: A cross‑departmental blind spot in AI growth policy — Planning, water, cooling, and grid demand

To the AI Energy Council secretariat (aiec@dsit.gov.uk),

I've submitted an independent, evidence‑based response to Ofgem's Curate consultation on data‑centre connections and queue management. This was a personal submission, not on behalf of any developer, DNO, vendor, or other stakeholder. One finding sits squarely in the kind of cross‑departmental gap the Council exists to close, rather than something Ofgem can act on alone, so I'm raising it with you directly.

The government's AI growth strategy is now a major force behind the demand filling the grid‑connections queue. That queue grew from roughly 41 GW to 125 GW in about a year, and data centres account for around 73 GW of it, by Ofgem's own count. A separate Ofgem press release cited a broader "at least 80 GW" figure; I use the consultation's project‑level number. But this growth ambition does not appear to be coordinated with the decisions that determine how much water and power AI infrastructure actually consumes once built.

Those decisions sit with local planning authorities, who choose, site by site, whether a data centre cools with water or with air. Even "closed‑loop" systems, now standard for AI compute, don't remove that choice: they run dry for most of the year, then draw water for evaporative assist once temperature rises, or simply because water is cheaper or more convenient — either way, trading water for a lower electricity draw, since water absorbs heat more efficiently than air. Climate change will make that trade‑off bind harder, not less: as heatwaves grow more frequent, more sites will spend more of the year drawing heavily on one resource or the other, at precisely the moment both are under pressure.

This is not a hypothetical concern. Parliament's Data Centres inquiry has already recorded this misalignment:

- Water UK's evidence (DCU0037) states that the government's AI Opportunities Action Plan "makes not a single mention of water," nor does its AI for Science Strategy.
- Energy UK's evidence (DCU0063) notes that "air cooling systems can be used in water‑constrained areas, but these use more energy than evaporative systems," and that there is "a lack of alignment in the timescales for securing all of the requirements necessary for a new development" across electricity, water, and planning.

This does not look like negligence. It looks like four bodies each doing their job well, but without a shared view of how their decisions interact. Ofgem sets the connection charge. NESO builds the demand forecast. Local planning decides the cooling mode. Central government sets the growth ambition driving demand into all three. None of the four, as far as the public record shows, tracks what the other three are doing to the numbers it relies on.

The Council already brings three of those four — DESNZ, Ofgem, and NESO — into the same room. Local planning is the missing voice, whether that means a seat on the Council or simply a route for planning decisions to feed back into the growth ambition the Council is coordinating.

I'd welcome the chance to share the fuller evidence behind this, including the parliamentary submissions cited above and a related finding on how NESO's long‑range demand forecast does not name climate change or cooling‑mode switching among its stated uncertainty drivers, if that would be useful as the Council's work continues.

Many thanks for your time, and for the Council's work on this.
